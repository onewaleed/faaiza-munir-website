On Sisters, Samosas, and Solving Murders: How My Family Inspired Arranged Murder

By Faaiza Munir • Dispatches from the Writing Desk

If you have ever attended a Pakistani family wedding, you will know that the investigative machinery of Scotland Yard has nothing on an auntie who wants to know why you aren’t married yet.

She will cross-examine you on your career, your travel schedule, your dietary preferences, and your third-cousin’s recent promotion in the space of four minutes—all while smiling warmly and pressing another plate of warm gulab jamun into your hands.

It was during one such wedding gathering in Edinburgh that the seed for Arranged Murder took root. I was sitting at a banquet table with my three real-life sisters. As usual, everyone had an opinion on everyone else’s outfit, two sisters were debating whether the groom looked trustworthy, and someone was trying to smuggle an extra platter of samosas to our table.

“What would happen if the drawing-room murder took place not in an isolated manor house, but in the middle of a chaotic, gossip-fuelled family gathering where everyone has a motive and nobody wants their samosas to get cold?”

Classic British murder mysteries have always been about human secrets behind polite facades. In British-Pakistani culture, family loyalty is fierce, but the banter is ruthless. When a body turns up, nobody panics silently. Everyone gathers around the kettle, multiple conflicting theories emerge, and the youngest sister is inevitably dispatched to listen at the door.

That is the spirit of the Asghar sisters: Amina’s spreadsheets, Zara’s sharp eye for fabric and flaws, Nadia’s morbid curiosity, and Shireen’s covert intelligence network. They fight constantly, but when danger threatens their family, they are completely unstoppable.
